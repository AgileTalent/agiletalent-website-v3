Performance Management for Nigerian Businesses

A useful performance process clarifies expectations, helps managers support employees and connects individual work to business priorities.

Performance management is often treated as an annual appraisal exercise. In practice, it is an ongoing cycle of agreeing expectations, checking progress, providing feedback and addressing barriers to effective work.

For growing organisations, a consistent approach can help managers make informed decisions about priorities, development and performance concerns. The process should be proportionate to the business and useful to the people expected to use it.

1. Start with business priorities

Clarify what the organisation or team needs to achieve before setting individual objectives. Translate those priorities into outcomes employees can influence. Each objective should explain the expected result, how progress will be assessed and when it will be reviewed.

Use measurable indicators where appropriate, but do not force every responsibility into a number. Quality, customer experience, collaboration, risk management and professional judgement may also matter.

2. Agree clear and realistic objectives

Employees should understand their responsibilities, how their work connects to team goals and which resources or decisions they depend on. Objectives should guide action while allowing for material changes in business priorities.

If the organisation uses weighted objectives, explain the weighting and assessment criteria in advance. Check that targets do not reward behaviour that undermines service quality, compliance or sound decision-making.

3. Make performance check-ins useful

Regular check-ins allow managers and employees to discuss progress, obstacles, workload and support. The frequency can vary by role and business cycle. A short, focused conversation is often more useful than a form completed without discussion.

Record important commitments, including who will take action and when. The purpose is to identify what is working and what needs attention, not to turn every conversation into a rating exercise.

4. Give specific, balanced feedback

Effective feedback describes an observable action or outcome, explains its impact and identifies what should continue or change. It should be timely, respectful and connected to agreed expectations.

Managers should invite the employee's perspective. A missed target may reflect unclear expectations, changing priorities, insufficient tools or a capability gap. Understanding the cause helps determine the appropriate response.

5. Use reviews to guide development

A formal review can summarise results over an agreed period, recognise contributions and identify development needs. Use relevant evidence rather than relying only on recent events or personal impressions. Employees should have an opportunity to provide context and discuss their goals.

Development actions should be practical. They may include coaching, training, job shadowing, clearer guidance or changes to work processes. Agree how progress will be reviewed.

6. Address underperformance fairly

When performance falls short, explain the gap between the agreed expectation and the observed result. Discuss possible causes, clarify the required improvement and agree reasonable support and review dates.

Keep appropriate records of expectations, conversations, support offered and outcomes. Apply the organisation's procedures consistently, and seek suitable HR or legal advice for sensitive cases.

7. Avoid common appraisal problems

  • Do not wait until year-end to discuss issues that could be addressed earlier.
  • Use clear criteria and relevant evidence rather than general impressions.
  • Train managers to set objectives and give constructive feedback.
  • Check that targets are realistic and within the employee's influence.
  • Keep forms proportionate and remove questions that do not inform a decision.
  • Review patterns across teams to identify inconsistent assessments or development needs.

What should a simple performance process include?

A practical cycle can include five stages: set objectives, check progress, review evidence, discuss results and agree development actions. Define the timing, roles and approval steps so employees and managers know what is expected.

Completion rates are useful, but they are not the only measure of success. Also consider the quality of objectives, usefulness of feedback, completion of development actions and whether the process supports better business outcomes.
